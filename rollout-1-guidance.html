Method / Rollout-1 guidance

How rollout-1 guidance is calculated

Guidance constructs a one-frame physics target from the current predicted video, measures a latent-space loss against that target, and uses its gradient to update the noisy sampling state. The pretrained bidirectional DiT and VAE stay fixed. “Rollout-1” means that the physics simulator advances the estimated ball positions and velocities by one video frame.

Settings used in the ten-video comparison

Video5 balls; 49 frames at 128 × 128 pixels
ConditioningFirst 2 latent frames, corresponding to 5 video frames
Reverse steps\(K\in\{10,20,50,100,200\}\); seed 0
Guidance windowStep indices 25 through \(K-1\), counting from zero
Inner update cap75 guidance updates per active denoising step
Frame to updateChoose the frame with the lowest local penalized Rollout-1 error above the step’s fixed threshold
ThresholdHalf the step’s initial minimum local error, with a floor of \(10^{-6}\)
Guidance strengthLinear decrease from 0.7 to 0.2 over the active steps
Loss and updateMSE at one target latent time slice; gradient through the DiT updates all future latent slices
SamplerFlow matching, shift 5, denoising strength 1; empty prompts and classifier-free guidance scale 1

At 10 or 20 reverse steps, guidance never activates because the run ends before step index 25. The settings above are shared by all ten cases.

The separate tuned Case 2 example uses the same guidance procedure with 50 updates per step and a final-step strength override of 0.10.

1. Form the current clean-video estimate

The input video is VAE-encoded, and its first two latent frames become the fixed conditioning. The remaining latent entries start from seeded Gaussian noise. The comparison launcher reproduces each vanilla run’s noise batch shape and selects the same sample position from that batch.

Let \(z\) be the current noisy latent at reverse-step index \(j\). Every inner guidance update begins with a fresh DiT evaluation at that step’s noise level:

\[v=v_\theta(z,t_j),\qquad \hat z_0=z-\sigma_jv,\qquad t_j=1000\sigma_j.\]

The code decodes \(\hat z_0\) with the VAE and replaces its first five RGB frames with the supplied conditioning frames. The resulting video is the input to the tracker. Decoding and target construction run outside the gradient graph.

2. Choose the frame to update: lowest error above the threshold

Track the balls in the current predicted video, then choose the frame to update with the lowest local Rollout-1 error above the stopping threshold. To calculate a frame’s error, simulate the balls forward from the preceding frame and compare those simulated positions with the current prediction. For example, the estimated positions and velocities at frame 12 provide the physics target for updating frame 13.

The tracker retains all ball detections, matches them to the five expected color identities, and records missing, wrong-color, and extra balls. Centers are converted to simulation coordinates. The preceding frame must belong to a consecutive block of at least three frames in which all five balls have their expected colors and no extra ball is detected.

Within each usable block, the code estimates velocities from position differences and applies its simulator-based velocity repair. At the block’s first frame, the initial estimate is the displacement to the third frame divided by two frame intervals. The simulator uses a 10 × 10 world, radius 0.7, radius adjustment −0.05, and a frame interval of \(1/15\) second.

For each candidate frame to update \(q\in\{5,\ldots,48\}\) (zero-based), simulate all five balls forward from frame \(q-1\) and compare the simulated centers with the detected centers at frame \(q\). Average the five Euclidean center errors to obtain that frame’s local score \(e_q\). A missing or wrong-color target ball receives the world-diagonal penalty \(\sqrt{200}\approx14.1421\); an extra target ball makes all five entries receive that penalty.

At the first inner iteration of each active denoising step, set the threshold below. Keep it fixed for the rest of that step, and select the smallest score still above it:

\[\tau_j=\max\!\left(0.5\min_{q\in S_{j,0}}e_q,\,10^{-6}\right),\qquad q^*\in\underset{q\in S:\,e_q>\tau_j}{\arg\min}\ e_q.\]

\(S_{j,0}\) is the set of candidate frames to update at the start of the step; \(S\) is the current set. Tracking and selection are repeated after every update. Exact ties are broken with a random generator seeded at 0. The same frame can be selected repeatedly.

3. Render and encode the one-frame physics target

First redraw the entire detected video as balls on the simulation background, retaining all detections, including extras. Copy that redraw and replace only the chosen frame to update, \(q^*\), with the five balls at their one-frame simulated positions. Every other reference frame remains the detection redraw.

Scale the reference RGB values to \([-1,1]\), then VAE-encode the complete 49-frame reference video with tiling disabled:

\[r=E_{\mathrm{VAE}}(x_{\mathrm{reference}}).\]

The encoded reference is detached and treated as a fixed target for this update. It is rebuilt from the newly predicted video on the next inner iteration. Its future target comes from the detected state and simulator.

4. Measure MSE at the target latent time slice

The latent shape is \(1\times16\times13\times16\times16\): batch, channels, time, height, width. The implementation maps the chosen zero-based video frame \(q^*\) to latent time index

\[\ell=\max\!\left(2,\left\lfloor\frac{q^*-1}{4}\right\rfloor+1\right).\]

It compares the clean prediction directly with the encoded reference at index \(\ell\), averaging over all 4,096 channel-and-spatial entries in that slice. The subtraction and squared-error reduction use float32:

\[L=\frac{1}{4096}\left\|\hat z_0[:,:,\ell,:,:]-r[:,:,\ell,:,:]\right\|_2^2.\]

The local physics score selects the target; this latent MSE supplies the gradient. The original Rollout-5 score shown under each video is computed separately from the final generated clip.

5. Differentiate through the DiT and update the noisy state

First differentiate \(L\) with respect to the clean prediction while holding the reference fixed. Then propagate that gradient through \(\hat z_0=z-\sigma_jv_\theta(z,t_j)\) to the noisy state:

\[g=\left(\frac{\partial\hat z_0}{\partial z}\right)^{\!\top}\nabla_{\hat z_0}L.\]

The gradient includes the DiT’s dependence on its input. Although the loss uses one latent time slice, the state update covers all future slices. Let \(P_F\) retain latent time indices 2 through 12 and zero the two conditioning slices. There are \(|F|=45{,}056\) future entries. The correction is

\[\begin{aligned} u&=\frac{P_Fg}{\max\!\left(\sqrt{\|P_Fg\|_2^2/|F|},\,10^{-6}\right)},\\[6pt] \gamma_j&=0.7+(0.2-0.7)\frac{j-25}{K-26},\qquad 25\le j\le K-1,\\[6pt] z^{\mathrm{new}}&=\operatorname{restore}_C\!\left( z-\gamma_j\,|\sigma_j-\sigma_{j+1}|\,\sqrt{L}\,u \right). \end{aligned}\]

The strength formula applies to the active runs, where \(K\ge50\). The code calculates the future-gradient RMS in float32, casts it back to the gradient’s dtype for division, and restores the conditioning latents after subtraction.

Each correction is applied directly. The updated state is detached before the next DiT evaluation. Continue at the same noise level until 75 updates have been applied, no candidate frame to update remains, or all candidate frames have errors at or below \(\tau_j\).

6. Take the denoising step and decode the final video

After the inner loop, evaluate the DiT at the final corrected state if that state has changed since the last evaluation. Use that velocity in the ordinary flow-scheduler update:

\[z_{j+1}=\operatorname{restore}_C\!\left( z_{\mathrm{end}}+(\sigma_{j+1}-\sigma_j)v_\theta(z_{\mathrm{end}},t_j) \right).\]

The terminal noise level is \(\sigma_K=0\). On inactive steps, the code makes one DiT evaluation and takes this scheduler step directly. After the last step, it VAE-decodes the latent, restores the first five observed RGB frames, and saves the resulting 49-frame video.
